# Autonomous Overtaking — Local Trajectory Planning Stack

A real-time motion planning and control stack for an autonomous vehicle that drives
on a two-lane rural road, recognizes a slower vehicle ahead, overtakes it through
the oncoming lane when safe, and then returns to its original lane and settles at
the lane center — all within a strict per-tick compute budget.

The ego vehicle is modeled after the **Renault Twizy** (2.34 m long, 1.69 m wheelbase),
cruising at up to ~60 km/h on a road with a 60 km/h speed limit while the blocked
lane traffic moves substantially slower.

The system runs as a closed loop:

1. **Perception (simulated)** provides the current world state: ego pose, lane geometry,
   and surrounding vehicles.
2. **Prediction** extrapolates each surrounding vehicle's future motion over the
   planning horizon (constant-velocity model).
3. **Planner** searches for the best feasible trajectory over the next few seconds.
4. **Controller** tracks the planned trajectory and produces steering-rate and
   acceleration commands.
5. The loop repeats every simulation tick, re-anchoring the goal ahead of the vehicle —
   there is no fixed destination; the planner continuously "drives toward the horizon".

Two runtime modes are supported: a deterministic sequential mode (plan → execute → replan)
and a threaded mode where the controller keeps tracking in real time while the planner
runs concurrently in its own process.

## How the Planner Works

The core planner is a **Hybrid A\*** search over discretized vehicle poses:

- **Motion primitives** generated by a kinematic bicycle model expand the search tree
  with dynamically feasible steering arcs rather than grid moves, so every candidate
  path is drivable by construction.
- A **Dubins-based heuristic** guides the search toward the goal region.
- Candidate trajectories are validated against predicted occupancy of other vehicles
  using oriented bounding boxes with safety margins.
- The search must return a trajectory within a hard **time budget (~2 s)** for a
  **6 s planning horizon**, which makes the cost function — not exhaustive search —
  the main tool for shaping behavior.

Because the planner explores a combinatorial space under time pressure, *what the
vehicle does* is largely decided by *what the cost function penalizes*. The next
section describes how the costs produce the three key behaviors: overtake, return,
and stay centered.

*The Hybrid A\* search core, motion primitives, and base cost terms were originally
developed by [Daniel](https://github.com/ds20031) as part of a university group
project; the overtaking and return-to-lane behavior shaping, performance work, and
surrounding architecture are extensions built on top of that foundation.*

## Shaping Behavior with Costs

Every candidate trajectory accumulates cost along its path. Four families of terms
work together to make the vehicle overtake, come back, and then hold the lane center.

### 1. Overtaking the slow vehicle

While a slower vehicle blocks the lane ahead, the planner must prefer leaving the
original lane over trailing behind:

- An **object force field** surrounds predicted vehicle footprints; cost grows
  sharply as the ego's inflated bounding box approaches another vehicle, effectively
  pushing the search around blockers instead of into them.
- The **target-speed cost** penalizes driving below the desired cruise speed, so a
  trajectory that stays stuck behind a 30 km/h lead vehicle accumulates speed-deficit
  cost every second.
- The base **opposite-lane penalty is intentionally mild** (0.5× multiplier): while a
  pass is in progress, occupying the oncoming lane is acceptable as long as it is
  collision-free against predicted oncoming traffic.

Together these make "pull out and pass" the cheapest survivable option once the
lead vehicle is slow enough.

### 2. Returning to the original lane

A pure penalty design fails here: if staying left is only mildly expensive, the
vehicle tends to linger beside or ahead of the passed car instead of coming back.
The stack therefore adds a **return-mode gate** on top of the static costs:

- Before each plan, the gate scans a forward corridor (~150 m along the travel
  direction, ±5.5 m wide). If no slower blocker is present ahead, the opposite-lane
  penalty is **scaled up ×5**.
- The result: during an active pass (blocker present) the oncoming lane is cheap;
  the moment the pass completes (blocker clear) it becomes expensive, and the
  cheapest trajectory bends back into the original lane.
- A **rear hysteresis margin (6 m)** keeps recently passed vehicles classified as
  blockers until they are safely behind, preventing the penalty from flipping on
  mid-pass and cutting the maneuver short.
- The gate evaluates the current scene only — flickering predictions cannot toggle
  the mode halfway through a pass.

### 3. Staying centered and comfortable

After re-entry, fine-grained terms take over:

- **Lane-center cost** (the heaviest single transition weight) grows with lateral
  offset from the current lane's centerline, pulling the vehicle back to y-center
  and keeping it there instead of drifting near lane edges.
- **Lane-yaw cost** aligns heading with the lane direction, eliminating small
  oscillations around the center.
- **Comfort costs** on longitudinal/lateral acceleration and jerk keep the executed
  maneuvers smooth — the overtake is a flowing S-curve, not a swerve.
- A **lane-exit cost** steeply penalizes any trajectory that leaves the drivable
  road surface entirely.

The balance is deliberate: strong enough center-pull that the vehicle converges to
~±0.5 m of the lane center after returning, weak enough that it never fights the
overtake while a blocker is present.

## Scenarios

| Scenario | Traffic | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `overtake_scenario` | 2 vehicles | Baseline: single slow lead + far oncomer; the canonical overtake-return-center run |
| `dense_overtake` | 7 vehicles | Stress test: platoon of slow vehicles in the ego lane plus staggered oncoming traffic |
| `simple_scenario` | 1 vehicle | Minimal traffic for controlled experiments |
| `no_objects_scenario` | none | Free-road sanity check |

In the baseline scenario the ego maintains full target speed throughout, passes the
slow lead vehicle with a comfortable clearance, re-enters its original lane, and
converges to the lane center without collisions across repeated runs.

## Future Work

- **Alternative planners** — implement RRT/RRT* behind the same planner interface and
  benchmark solution quality vs. Hybrid A* under identical cost functions and time budgets.
- **Richer scenarios** — multi-lane highways, stop-and-go traffic, cut-in maneuvers,
  partially occluded oncoming vehicles, and varying speed limits.
- **Controller upgrades** — extend the MPC with obstacle-aware constraints and richer
  vehicle dynamics; evaluate a PID baseline on the same trajectories to quantify
  what predictive control buys.
- **Prediction improvements** — replace constant-velocity prediction with learned or
  interaction-aware models and measure planner robustness to prediction error.
